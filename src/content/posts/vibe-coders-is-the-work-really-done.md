We live in the age of vibe coding, with agents running around getting work done. But is it really "done"?

![3D letters spelling AI](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1677442136019-21780ecad995?w=1600&q=80&auto=format&fit=crop)
<figcaption>Agents everywhere, getting work “done” · Photo via <a href="https://unsplash.com">Unsplash</a></figcaption>

All of a sudden we've become 10x engineers, for a ~$20 payment to OpenAI, Anthropic, or whichever company has the logo we like best.

## How Work Changed

![An empty modern office hallway](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1497366216548-37526070297c?w=1600&q=80&auto=format&fit=crop)
<figcaption>Fewer people, more output · Photo via <a href="https://unsplash.com">Unsplash</a></figcaption>

At my company, most engineers don't write code anymore. About a year ago, they wrote (buggy) code, then read it, reviewed it, refactored it and tested it. Now they just type a prompt into a chat box. It goes to an AI company's server, the AI writes the code, tests it, and that's it. Done.

Even after a layoff that hit half the company, product managers can't give us enough tasks. Why? Because within a couple of months, we started working at least 10x faster, with **acceptable quality** code.

And the AI's code really is acceptable. It's not bad at all. It considers security, makes good choices about extensibility, and maybe even thinks about the future. Scalability isn't scary anymore, even for a fresh junior.

As software engineers, we've come to see that AI is more than a coding helper or a good brainstorming partner. It actually gets work done.

That said, we're not exactly sitting idle when the task queue runs dry. We push further and use the freed-up time on things that wouldn't have crossed our minds before, like building a company-wide knowledge base anyone can query, or using AI to spot malicious users in our apps. That's where we let our quality do the talking.

## The Problem with "Acceptable"

![Close-up of code on a monitor](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1461749280684-dccba630e2f6?w=1600&q=80&auto=format&fit=crop)
<figcaption>Readable, extensible, scalable… and does it work? · Photo via <a href="https://unsplash.com">Unsplash</a></figcaption>

But there's one thing I'm not sure about: that the AI's work is "acceptable."

No matter how readable, extensible or scalable the code is, all the product cares about is that the thing works as intended. If it doesn't work, what's happening behind the scenes doesn't matter. And funnily enough, if it does work, what's happening behind the scenes doesn't matter either. If pressing the green button takes $20 from the user's account and an invoice gets processed somewhere, who cares whether it was written in Go, Rust or Assembly?

Of course, it's always been like this. But as developers, it looks like we're not slowly letting go of the back end of the work. We're dropping it completely. I don't see this as a mistake so much as a misconception.

## AI Can't Invent

![A glowing circuit board](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1550751827-4bd374c3f58b?w=1600&q=80&auto=format&fit=crop)
<figcaption>Pattern matching, not invention · Photo via <a href="https://unsplash.com">Unsplash</a></figcaption>

Here's my thesis: **AI can't invent anything.** It only applies the solution it saw during training that's most similar to your problem. It builds your feature based on the most similar feature it knows. If your work needs a small but revolutionary idea (and you can't know that until you get into the code), doing it with AI actually closes the door on a lot of opportunities.

In the end, you get:

- A codebase you can't understand, even when you read it.
- Lots of mini-features the AI assumed you needed, which you'll never use and which slow your program down.
- A pile of technical debt, and the scalability problems that come with it.
- A dopamine addiction from watching reels for minutes while you wait for the AI to respond.

## Why We Take the Easy Path

![A food delivery courier riding down a narrow street](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1526367790999-0150786686a2?w=1600&q=80&auto=format&fit=crop)
<figcaption>The $10 shortcut · Photo via <a href="https://unsplash.com">Unsplash</a></figcaption>

But humans are very rational creatures. If you want to check your phone every minute, eat more sugar than you should, speed for no reason, or use AI even to reply to a coworker, the reason is the same, even when you know it's wrong: you're picking the easiest path to happiness.

So instead of spending days on a task, solving tiny sub-problems, testing it when you think it finally works, and starting over when it doesn't give the expected result, of course it makes more sense to tell the AI "handle this task for me, bro," lean back, and watch it do the job in minutes.

To put it another way: when you can order food for $10 in a few minutes, why would it make sense to go grocery shopping, learn a recipe, cook, put the leftovers in containers, and then wash those containers when they're empty? You could even ask your Claude subscription to order the food for you 🤨

## Satisfaction, Ownership and Growth

![Two people cooking together in a kitchen](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1556910103-1c02745aae4d?w=1600&q=80&auto=format&fit=crop)
<figcaption>The long way, and the one you can call your own · Photo via <a href="https://unsplash.com">Unsplash</a></figcaption>

For me, the main problem in this equation is the feeling of satisfaction. How can you own work you didn't do? How can you enjoy it? And more importantly, how can you improve yourself?

That's what I want to talk about in this post.

I think proactivity is very important for a developer's career. I've spent most of my 5+ years of experience thinking of things and acting on them without being asked: trying to build what customers or managers wanted before they even knew they wanted it, or doing more than the task asked and thinking about the problems it would cause later.

That was true before AI became this widespread too. So let's look at how we can stay proactive from now on.

## How to Stay Proactive with AI

### 1. Don't expect a task from a single prompt

The biggest mistake is expecting the AI to finish a task from one prompt. It kills our sense of satisfaction and ownership, and it also hurts the AI's results. It's like starting to write code the moment you've read a task.

### 2. Plan and design first

![A hand arranging a flow diagram on a whiteboard](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1531403009284-440f080d1e12?w=1600&q=80&auto=format&fit=crop)
<figcaption>Design it yourself, then let the AI type · Photo via <a href="https://unsplash.com">Unsplash</a></figcaption>

First, have it make a plan. Think hard about the work and do some system design. This way we increase our ownership instead of losing it, and we can say, "I designed this code."

What we end up with is a system design we made ourselves, using the AI only as a tool for bouncing ideas around. After that, there's no harm in letting the AI write the code.

### 3. Context switch while the AI writes

While the AI writes the code, I find it very reasonable to switch to something else, and I feel more productive that way.

While doing a task for a customer, you could build a tool that makes your team's work easier, think of a fix for an efficiency problem you noticed on the team, or just read a book about threads in Java. Any of these is definitely better than waiting.

### 4. Review it like a human wrote it

![A laptop showing code next to a yellow mug](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1517694712202-14dd9538aa97?w=1600&q=80&auto=format&fit=crop)
<figcaption>Coffee, focus, and a suspicious review · Photo via <a href="https://unsplash.com">Unsplash</a></figcaption>

Of course, it doesn't end with the AI writing the code. I like to grab a coffee, focus, and review the code with suspicion, as if a person had written it. That increases ownership even more.
