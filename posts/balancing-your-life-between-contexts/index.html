Balancing Your Life Between Contexts

time-managementcontext-switchingmindlifework-life-balance

Each individual has something to do. A person can be engineering student, he/she needs to study frequently and achieve some goals during his/her educational life. When this person graduates and become an engineer, let’s say mining engineer: A mining engineer’s day generally starts at the office, plans for that day’s work and priorities, and goes down to the field. Then he/she manages certain projects according to his position, and may encounter problems that block him/her. For example, there may be a machine failure, personal problems may arise etc. A standard work day is similar for everyone, depending on the industry.

When our engineer in scenario completes the working day, it is likely that there will be other tasks waiting for him. A freelance job, mentoring, education, hobbies etc. Basically, the day has a non-work section. In this section, unlike working time, you are the person receiving service rather than serving.

This article will help you to make the strict separation between work and life in your day better by expanding the topics I mentioned in my related article Packaging The Life.

Switching context in a healthy way

In my daily life, I am an engineering student, and as of the time I am writing this article, I have about 4 months left until my graduation. In addition, I work as a software developer in a company for few years. I also spend the remaining time from both contexts to learn new things and improve myself in several areas (mostly in software).

Therefore, my responsibilities during a regular day include fulfilling my duties at the company, seeking support from my colleagues in case of any obstacles, attending my classes at school without missing any, and organizing meetings for group projects related to one of my courses and collaborating with a few individuals to develop a shared project. As a result of that, I need to switch between different contexts at various times throughout my daily life. Although I made a few mistakes at the beginning, I gained experience in this regard.

In Turkish, there is a saying “soluklanmak” (to take a breath), which means taking a break to rest before continuing with a task or starting a new one. I also believe that it is necessary to take a short break before switching from one context to another. Let’s say we are transitioning from Context Purple to Context Brown. Context Purple could be a complex task that took a lot of time and effort, or it could be a simple activity that didn’t take much time and energy. Regardless of the type of task, it is important to take a context-free break before moving on to Context Brown.

In this context-free break, what you choose to do is up to you. If you need to change locations to transition to Context Brown, you can choose activities that do not strain your mind and help you distance yourself from the previous context, such as reading a book or listening to a podcast. If you don’t need to change locations, such as when attending an online meeting, you can take a short walk, prepare a drink for yourself, or spend time with your cat, for example.

Indeed, taking a context-free break will not have a direct impact on the previous context, but it can provide some relief and help clear your mind before transitioning to the next context, especially if it is a demanding one. If you make a habit of taking these breaks as someone who transitions between multiple contexts throughout the day, it may increase your overall productivity in the other contexts, even though this is a qualitative observation rather than a quantitative one.

In summary, as I mentioned in my previous article, it’s important to package time periods. Contexts can be a good example of time periods that need to be packaged in our minds. It’s logical to put them in a package and close it when it’s time, and it can be opened again later, but we shouldn’t allow a package to occupy our mind and bother us in the future.

This isn’t a situation that can be solved with a tool or a simple spell. The barrier between contexts should start in our minds.
